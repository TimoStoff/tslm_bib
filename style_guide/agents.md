# Scientific figure guidance

Use these instructions when designing, reviewing, or revising scientific figures. They summarize
the visual-communication principles in Nature Reviews' *Guide to designing figures* and
*Scientific illustration for commissioned content*. The source chiefly concerns conceptual and
explanatory figures; apply its general principles to data figures without overriding a journal's
specific submission requirements.

Source: [Nature Reviews artwork guide](https://www.nature.com/documents/natrev-artworkguide.pdf).

## Start with the scientific message

Before drawing, state in one sentence what the reader should learn from the figure. Identify the
essential objects, relationships, or changes needed to support that message. Remove anything that
does not improve understanding, provide necessary context, or support interpretation.

Use a figure when spatial relationships, a process, a hierarchy, or a phenomenon are important.
Use a table or list when the content only categorizes items and contains no meaningful visual
relationship or action. Do not disguise a table as a figure with decorative icons.

## Establish the reading order

- Prefer a clear left-to-right or top-to-bottom flow, beginning near the upper left.
- Use a circular layout only for a genuine cycle or another familiar circular structure with a
  clear focal point.
- Use panel letters and short subheadings to establish structure in multi-panel figures.
- Arrange causes before effects, inputs before transformations, and abstractions before their
  concrete realization unless the scientific narrative requires another order.
- Keep related elements close together and align repeated structures consistently.

The visual hierarchy must match the information hierarchy. Give the central mechanism or result
the strongest emphasis. Render supporting context with less detail and more neutral colour. Do not
give every element equal visual weight.

## Edit visually

Treat figure revision like prose editing. Ask, in order:

1. What is the essential message?
2. Is any information needed to understand it missing?
3. What can be removed without weakening it?
4. Is any idea encoded more than once?
5. Is any decoration competing with scientific information?

Combine redundant stages where this makes a process easier to follow. Avoid overcrowding; a
full-page figure will rarely need more than about six panels. Break a dense figure into separate
figures when its labels, objects, or relationships cannot remain legible at publication size.

Keep lists short and grouped. Approximately five items per group is a useful upper target for
material that readers must remember together.

## Define every visual element

- Label the first instance of each object, symbol, line style, and relationship.
- Explain all encodings either directly or in a compact legend.
- Use words instead of an image when the image would be an ambiguous or space-consuming label.
- Minimize abbreviations. Define every abbreviation that remains.
- Use one arrow style for one meaning. If arrow colour, weight, head, or line pattern changes, the
  distinction must be explained and scientifically meaningful.
- Make direction explicit. Avoid ambiguous arrows between objects when a single process arrow or
  labelled relationship would be clearer.
- Use the same colour, shape, orientation, and ordering for the same concept within and across
  related figures.

## Use typography consistently

- Aim for 8 pt text at final figure size and never use text smaller than 7 pt in a draft.
- Use one legible sans-serif family unless the venue requires another typeface.
- Use bold text for headings or deliberate emphasis. Do not use arbitrary font-size changes or
  italics to manufacture hierarchy.
- Prefer black or near-black text. Avoid coloured text unless colour is itself part of the encoding
  and contrast remains sufficient.
- Keep capitalization, terminology, units, and numeric precision consistent.
- In equations, set variables in italics, constants in roman type, and vectors in bold.

## Use colour to communicate

Use a restrained, accessible palette. Colour should establish hierarchy, group related elements,
or follow a meaningful scientific convention. It should not merely make adjacent objects look
different.

- Give the main focus the most saturated accent colours.
- Render contextual or background elements in shared neutral tones.
- Reuse colours consistently across the paper.
- Do not rely on colour alone; reinforce categories with labels, position, shape, or line pattern.
- Avoid red–green contrasts as the only distinction.
- Check contrast and simulate common forms of colour-vision deficiency.
- Keep text readable against every fill and background.

The Nature guide does not specify reusable colour values. Follow an established project palette
when one exists, while preserving these hierarchy and accessibility requirements.

## Draw only useful imagery

Use illustrations and icons when they provide orientation, spatial context, or scientific meaning.
Do not add icons solely for decoration. Detailed imagery belongs in the focal area; contextual
objects should usually be simplified.

Represent natural objects realistically when their appearance is scientifically relevant. When
they serve only as landmarks, simplify them enough that they do not compete with the main action.

## Prepare data figures carefully

- Label every axis and include units.
- Provide a key for colours, markers, symbols, and line styles.
- Use comparable axis ranges and ordering when panels invite comparison.
- Show uncertainty, sample size, and statistical meaning when relevant.
- Do not use a graph when a small table or direct annotation communicates the values more clearly.
- Avoid publication-count trends and other visually quantitative material that lacks scientific
  relevance to the argument.

The Nature Reviews source discourages original single-study data in its commissioned review
content. Treat that as article-type guidance, not a universal prohibition for research papers.

## Preserve editability and provenance

- Prefer vector output for diagrams, text, and line art.
- Keep text, arrows, and labels editable and on separate layers from photographs or scans.
- Supply photographs without flattened labels when possible.
- For print raster imagery, use at least 300 dpi at final size unless the venue specifies more.
- Record the source and license of every adapted or reproduced element.
- Do not assume that an image found online is reusable.
- Follow the journal's permissions process rather than contacting publishers independently when
  the journal explicitly manages permissions.

Scientific correctness takes priority over polish. A clear sketch with complete content is a
better starting point than a polished but ambiguous figure.

## Lessons from the guide's examples

The source examples are best treated as design tests rather than templates:

- **Focus through hierarchy:** the focal mechanism receives saturated accents and greater detail,
  while contextual structures share muted background tones.
- **Before-and-after process diagram:** a cluttered version repeats stages and uses ambiguous
  arrows; the improved version merges redundant steps and expresses the action with one clear
  directional arrow.
- **The “faux figure” test:** a collection of barriers and solutions decorated with icons remains a
  list. Removing the icons and presenting a table or grouped list improves comprehension.
- **The overcrowding test:** many small panels and tiny labels produce cognitive load. Fewer,
  larger panels with at least 7 pt text communicate more effectively.

Do not copy these examples literally. Apply the underlying edit: clarify the message, remove
redundancy, and make the visual encoding explicit.

## Final review checklist

Before delivering a figure, verify that:

- its message can be stated in one sentence;
- the reading order is apparent without explanation;
- every object and encoding is defined;
- every arrow has one clear source, destination, and meaning;
- repeated concepts use identical visual treatment;
- the focal information is visually dominant;
- no colour is decorative or the sole carrier of meaning;
- red–green distinctions and low-contrast text have been avoided;
- all labels remain legible at final size;
- panels, lists, and legends contain no unnecessary material;
- axes, units, symbols, and statistical quantities are complete;
- photographs and raster images meet the required final-size resolution;
- editable source files and provenance records are retained; and
- the figure still agrees with the manuscript and caption.
